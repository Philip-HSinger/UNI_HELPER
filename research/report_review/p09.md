## Part 1: In character (reply email to Philip)

Subject: Re: Calliper sample report — a few numbered questions

Hi Philip,

Thanks for sending this. For context, I'm a former developer who now runs a small advising practice, and I write a lot about why "multiply the per-school chances" is the wrong way to read a college list. So you're preaching to the choir, and that's exactly why I read it closely. Long email ahead; it's meant as a compliment.

**What convinces me**

1. **You're asking the right question.** Families always want "the number" for each school, and I've never had a defensible way to show them that eight separate numbers say little about the one outcome they actually fear. Page 2's three boxes (25% naive, why that's wrong, 40% linked) is the clearest way I've seen it put on paper. I'd show that panel to a parent.
2. **The arithmetic adds up.** The naive product is 0.87 x 1.00 x 0.98 x 0.97 x 0.93 x 0.92 x 0.79 x 0.46 = 0.253, and odds.json agrees (0.2528). The essay table sums correctly: 2,700 words in total, 1,576 new, 41.6% saved. The URI result makes sense. A ~95% school caps the risk near 5% whatever the correlation is, and your linked 4.1% sits sensibly above the naive 1.4%.
3. **The Tufts decision tree is the part I'd pay for.** Re-planning after ED is where I lose the most hours. My spreadsheet takes an evening per student in mid-December, and I have deferral conversations on the calendar right now (one family has already asked "what if Sam is deferred, which RD schools do we add?"). A tool that re-scores every pending school once an ED result is in is exactly what I want.
4. **You state your assumptions.** The ED rate halfway between overall and ED on the log-odds scale, the stale CDS years flagged (Holy Cross 2023–24), "forecasts not yet tested against real outcomes", the "not yet included" list. That honesty buys a lot of credit with me. You even flag that the deferral-as-near-miss assumption is the one most worth checking. Good instinct.
5. **The essay-chain idea is clever.** I wouldn't buy for it, but the chain from Tufts to Penn to Emory to Rice is useful, and students would like it.

**What I think is wrong or overstated**

1. **The correlation is assumed, not measured.** Everything on page 2 rests on how much of the "read" is shared across schools. odds.json shows `gamma_shared: 0.3` and `rho_th: 0.4`, and the report never says where those come from or what pairwise decision correlation they imply. Going from 25% to 40% is a 60% relative increase that depends entirely on one hand-set parameter. I need to see the risk at γ = 0, 0.15, 0.3 and 0.5. Without that, "the real answer" overstates what you know.
2. **GPA is left out, so the 40% is probably too high for a real client.** You call John "strong grades", but the model never uses grades. Because GPA and rigor are unknown to the model, they end up in the shared "how the application reads" factor and inflate the correlated uncertainty. For my own clients I know their GPA, rigor and the quality of their recommendations. Most of what your model treats as shared unknowns is known to me, so the true correlated risk would be lower and the ranges narrower. I'd also guess this is why Fordham comes out at only 54% for a 1420 with strong grades, against a 47% overall admit rate. Most counselors would say higher.
3. **The intervals mean two different things.** The per-school "80% likely range" is enormous: Fordham runs from 9% to 94%, Tufts from 1% to 41%. Yet the list-level risk gets a tight "34% to 43%". If the per-school ranges come from the shared read, which you've already averaged over to get 40%, then the 34–43% must be something else (ED-rate sensitivity? parameter uncertainty?). Please label it. Also, the 40% sits near the top of 34–43%, which looks odd without an explanation.
4. **There's a bug in the Penn interval.** In odds.json, Penn's baseline chance is 0.354%, but its range is 0.183% to 0.279%: the point estimate falls outside its own interval. The same happens in the rejected branch (0.207% against 0.183% to 0.195%). The chart hides it because Penn is drawn as a bare dot, but if I found it in five minutes, other quant-minded counselors will too.
5. **The deferral branch looks thin.** By the law of total probability, the tree implies P(Tufts reject) ≈ 80% and P(defer) ≈ 6.5% (0.065 x 14% + 0.80 x 48% ≈ 40%). Is 6–7% of ED applicants deferred consistent with Tufts' actual practice? Your deferral band drives the "risk falls to 14%, keep the list as it is" advice, and that advice is the riskiest sentence in the report. Telling a family to keep the list as it is after a deferral is the opposite of what most of us would say. I'd want real evidence before saying it.
6. **The URI headline is just "add a safety".** "One application ... would bring that to 4%" is true, but any IEC would tell John to add a 75%-admit school. The model isn't needed for that. Its value is in quantifying the trade-off and the post-ED update. The headline undersells that and makes the tool sound obvious.
7. **"Five strong links let John borrow 42%" isn't right.** The 42% comes from all 11 links in the plan, not five. It's minor, but I notice it. There's also an irony. Your correlation comes from "every school reads the same essays", so recycling 42% of the words should, by your own logic, raise the correlation between decisions. Does the model account for that?
8. **There's no evidence yet.** I'm not asking for a published validation study, but "not yet tested against real outcomes" means I'd be beta-testing on real families. That's fine if I'm told so up front and can test it myself.
9. **"Two independent scorers" with "correlation 0.89 on the pairs that matter".** Picking the subset after the fact flatters the agreement. What's the agreement on all 393 pairs? And are the scorers people or LLM agents? (The README says agents. That's fine, but say so plainly.)
10. **It doesn't say where client data goes.** That's my hard line. The report shows a web-style UI mock-up and says nothing about where student names and data are processed. The README mentions a Python `AdmissionModel` toolkit. If it runs locally, lead with that.

**Questions for you (numbered, as promised)**

1. Where do γ = 0.3 and ρ = 0.4 come from? What pairwise correlation do they imply for, say, Tufts and BU? Can you send risk-of-zero sensitivity across γ?
2. What exactly is the "34% to 43%" interval?
3. How is a deferral defined in the latent model (a band below the cutoff? what width?), and what deferral share does that imply compared with Tufts' published or observed rates?
4. Can I input GPA, rigor, or my own rating of the file, so the shared uncertainty shrinks to what's actually unknown?
5. Does it run locally (pip-installable, or a notebook) so client names never leave my laptop? Is the source readable?
6. Can I batch-update: load all 28 seniors, enter mid-December ED/EA results from a CSV, and get every list re-scored?
7. How does it handle two early results arriving together (Tufts ED and Fordham EA both land around Dec 15–20)? Your tree still treats Fordham as pending.
8. Can I backtest on my past three cohorts (~80 students, ~700 decisions, de-identified)? With that sample I can't validate a 40% zero-admit tail directly, but I can check calibration and conditional rates like P(admit at B | rejected at A) against independence. Will you help set that up?
9. Can you send the annotated copy you mention ("full derivation of every number")?
10. What's the price after the trial, and is there a per-student or practice tier?

**Would I trial it?** Yes, this season, on my ED-deferred and ED-rejected students, if it runs locally or with de-identified inputs. I'd run it in parallel with my spreadsheet, not in front of families yet.

**Would I pay?** Not on this report alone. I would pay the same day if (a) I see the methods manual with the correlation parameters justified, (b) it runs locally, and (c) a backtest on my own past clients shows it adds information over base-rate multiplication, especially the post-ED updates. A free trial through May would let me get all three done this cycle.

**Price:** $390/yr is trivial for me. I spend about $1,500/yr on software. I'd pay up to roughly $600/yr for a solo practice if it replaces my December spreadsheet evenings. Quality is what I'm sensitive to, not price. Don't discount to win me over; earn it with the validation.

Best,
Jonathan
