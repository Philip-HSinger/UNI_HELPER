# Report review: p03, Director of College Counseling, independent boarding school (CT)

Persona: Eleanor Whitcombe, Director of College Counseling, Hawthorne Hill School (about 420 boarders, 5 counselors).
Report reviewed: `10_demo/outputs/Calliper_report_John_Smith.pdf` (7 pages), handed over after a short in-person meeting with the author, a Brown freshman.

## Part 1: In character

*Notes to file, later shared with Ben Okafor (Associate Director)*

Ben,

A young man from Brown, Philip Singer, stopped by after the regional breakfast and left me the attached sample report from his project, "Calliper." I read it on the train home. I am writing my thoughts down now, partly because I will not have a free hour again until January, and partly because one idea in it is better than I expected.

**What I found persuasive**

The central point on page 2 is correct, and I have never seen it explained so cleanly. Multiplying a student's chances school by school gives a 25% chance of no admissions. The real figure is closer to 40%, because every school reads the same transcript, the same letters and the same essays. If Tufts reads him as weaker than we hoped, Boston College probably will too. That is exactly what I try to tell parents who ask why "eight good schools" is not a safe list, and I usually fail. The three-box graphic ("The obvious answer / Why that is wrong / The real answer") is the clearest version of that argument I have seen. I could imagine using a version of it at the winter parent night, without any student's numbers on it.

I also like the page 4 tree for "When Tufts decides." Its practical message is right: a December rejection should trigger a list change before January, and a rejection is the one result that needs action. Ben, our ED2 memo says this less precisely. I was glad the author says plainly, in his own text, that the deferral branch is the part of the model "most worth checking against real outcomes." Many counselors, me included, read an ED deferral as a soft no, and he admits his model treats it the other way. That candour counts for something.

The "cheapest fix" idea on page 3 is sound counseling in a new form. You add a likely school that asks for no new writing, and you add it now rather than in a panic on December 16th. URI for a Rhode Island student is precisely what I would suggest.

**What confuses me**

- Is this meant for the student, the parents or the counselor? It is written to all three at once. The first sentence, "John's list has a 40% chance of no admissions," is a sentence I would never allow a family to read without a counselor in the room. I cannot tell whether families would ever see this screen.
- Where are grades, rigor, the school profile and the counselor letter? The model works from SAT scores and published admit rates. At page 7 I learn that GPA is "not yet included." For a school like ours, the transcript in context and our letters are most of the application. A model that leaves them out is estimating some other student.
- "Penn: under 1%." I understand the logic, but a 1420 at Penn is not under 1% for every student. A recruited athlete or a first-generation student with a remarkable story could do better. Saying "under 1%" with such precision about a holistic process is the kind of claim I spend conference panels warning against.
- "Checked a second time by an independent agent" and "two independent scorers": were these people or software? I suspect software. If so, say so plainly.
- "The full derivation of every number is in the annotated copy." I was not given the annotated copy.

**What I think is wrong or risky**

1. **Raw probabilities reach families.** This is my firmest objection. Parents paying full boarding tuition already argue with every "reach" label. A number like "54%" gives them something precise to argue with, and something to quote back to us when the result goes the other way. "Odds" make anxious families more anxious, not less. Without a counselor-only layer and banded language, I could not let this near our families.
2. **The essay section reads as gaming.** "Five strong links let John borrow 42% of his January essay words." I have spent years asking admissions deans what they want, and they all say the same thing: a "Why us" essay written for them. The plan has "Why Rice" taking 53% of its words from "Why BU." A dean who notices that does not think "efficient," she thinks "generic." The underlying idea is a good one: writing the essays in a sensible order so that thinking carries forward. Framed as a word-count saving, though, I could not defend it to a dean, our Head or a trustee. My name is on our school's relationship with those offices.
3. **A real school's name is on a fictional student.** "Every school is on Providence Country Day's 2024–2026 acceptance list." That is a real school, and I know people there. Does PCD know its list is being used to sell a product? I would want to know before I said a word about this to anyone.
4. **Privacy is not addressed at all.** The report contains nothing on what leaves the school, where it is stored or who can see it. The only contact is "calliper@agentmail.to" and "@philip." Our Director of Technology is collecting our vendor data inventory by December 5th. A tool with no privacy documentation, no company name and no data addendum would not get past his first question. If it needs students' names to leave campus, it does not get past him at all.
5. **It is untested.** "The forecasts themselves have not yet been tested against real outcomes." I respect the honesty, but our own eight years of outcomes, read on Scoir scattergrams, are a tested model for our students. National Common Data Set rates do not account for our profile, our letters or our relationships with these offices. Until this tool beats our spreadsheet on our own history, it has not earned a place.
6. **It was built for a different school.** The example student is a single Providence day student with a short regional list. Our students are boarders from thirty states and a dozen countries, and many are international, without US residency for in-state "likely" schools like URI. The example does not look like our students.

**What I would ask the author**

- Who is the reader: the counselor, the family or the student? Can the counselor turn off the percentages, or decide what the family sees?
- Will you test this on our eight years of de-identified outcomes, before we commit to anything, and show me where it would have been wrong?
- What exactly would leave our school? Can it run on a student code instead of a name, with no transcripts? Who stores the data, where, and for how long? Will you sign our data addendum?
- Does Providence Country Day know its acceptance list appears on your sample?
- How do you handle GPA, rigor, international students, athletes and legacies, the students who make up much of our list-building?
- Have any peer schools used this, and may I speak with their director?
- Who is Calliper as an organisation? Is there a company, an advisor or a faculty sponsor?

**Would I pilot it, pay for it, or pass it on?**

- **Pilot:** Not this season. We adopt nothing between November and January, and I would not experiment on this year's seniors in the middle of ED. I would consider a **summer pilot** under three conditions: it is run on our de-identified history first, it is counselor-only, and no names leave campus. That is a genuine "perhaps," which is more than I give most vendor mail.
- **Pay:** Not yet. If a summer back-test showed it agrees with our outcomes as well as our own counselors do, and it helped us explain list balance to families without giving them raw odds, $1,000 to $2,000 a year would be within what I can approve. Our associate director spends more than that in staff time each winter defending lists to parents.
- **Pass on:** To you, Ben, with this note, and with page 2 flagged as something we might adapt for parent night with no student numbers on it. I would not pass it to the Head or to any family in its current form. I might mention the correlated-decisions idea to the regional association's spring panel on AI in admissions, as a question, not an endorsement.

Please file this for our June tools review. If Mr. Singer writes, a courteous reply saying we would welcome a conversation in May would be appropriate.

Warm regards,
Eleanor

## Part 2: Out of character, the five changes that would most raise this persona's willingness to act

Ranked by how much each removes a dealbreaker or answers her main objection.

### 1. Make it counselor-facing, with banded language for families instead of raw odds (removes dealbreaker 1)
- Replace the opening line, "John's list has a 40% chance of no admissions, and one application to a school he has not considered would bring that to 4%," with:
  **"Counselor view, not for families. John's list has no school he is very likely to be admitted to. Because colleges read the same file, his chances rise and fall together, so the list is riskier than it looks school by school. Adding one likely school that asks for no new essays (URI) closes most of that gap."**
- Add a two-panel design on page 1: on the left, "What the counselor sees" (percentages and ranges); on the right, "What the family sees," a list-balance bar with **Likely / Possible / Reach / Far reach** bands and no numbers. Add a footer on every page: "Percentages are visible only to counselors. Families see bands, shared by their counselor."
- On page 2, relabel the chart "Only Fordham is better than even odds" as "Where John's list sits," with the band names shown alongside the dots.

### 2. Add a validation plan against the school's own history (answers "our eight years beat national data")
- Replace "the forecasts themselves have not yet been tested against real outcomes" with a boxed section:
  **"Before you use it with a single student: send us 5–8 years of de-identified outcomes (a Scoir export with names removed). We will show you, school by school, how often Calliper's bands matched what actually happened, and where your counselors' calls did better. Free; takes about two weeks; you keep the results."**
- Add an empty sample calibration chart labelled "Your school's results go here" to show what she would receive.

### 3. Add a one-page privacy and data-handling statement and a real organisational identity (removes dealbreakers 2 and 3)
- Add a page titled "What leaves your school" with these lines: **"No student names. Students are identified by a code your office assigns. Inputs: test scores, GPA band, college list, essay prompts, nothing else. Stored in [region], encrypted, deleted 30 days after the season or on request. We sign your school's data addendum. No data is sold or used to train models for others. Security summary available for your Director of Technology."**
- Replace "Calliper · Philip Singer · calliper@agentmail.to" with a named entity, a school-domain address, and a faculty or industry advisor if there is one.
- Remove "Providence Country Day's 2024–2026 acceptance list" unless the school has given written permission. Otherwise use: "every school is one a student with this profile could realistically apply to."

### 4. Reframe the essay section from "borrowing words" to "writing order," and never reuse "Why us" essays (removes the "gaming" reputational risk)
- Change the heading "Five strong links let John borrow 42% of his January essay words" to **"Write in this order: start where his thinking carries furthest."**
- Change the essay intro to: **"Every essay is still written for its own prompt. The plan only chooses the order, so ideas he has already developed can be deepened instead of reinvented. 'Why this college' essays are always written from scratch."**
- Remove the BU "why BU" to Rice "why Rice" link (53%) from the table and the matrix, and replace "Share covered / New words" with "Ideas to build on" and an estimated hours saved.

### 5. Add a closing "summer pilot for independent schools" page that fits her calendar and her peers
- Exact wording: **"Calliper pilots with independent schools from June to August, never during the application season. A pilot is: (1) a de-identified back-test on your outcomes, (2) a counselor-only workspace for 2–3 counselors, (3) a parent-night explainer on why lists fail together, with no student data in it. No cost for the pilot; afterwards, under $2,000 a year per school. Schools piloting in 2027: [names, with permission]."**
- Add a simple adapted example for a boarding student (an international or out-of-state student with no in-state safety school), so the report no longer "sounds like it was built for a different kind of school."
