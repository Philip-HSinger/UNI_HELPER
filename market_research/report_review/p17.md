## Part 1: In character (a reply to the author before the call)

Subject: Re: Calliper sample report (John Smith), notes before we talk

Dear Philip,

Thank you for the report. I read all seven pages on the Saturday after Thanksgiving, which tells you two things: it held my attention, and I have no life in December.

I'll be honest about the shape of my reading. I skimmed pages 1 to 4, all those odds and Bayesian branches. I'm a nonfiction writer, not an actuary, and my students' parents already have enough people telling them percentages. I slowed down at page 4, where the word "Essays" appears, and I read pages 5 and 6 twice. That's the product, as far as I'm concerned. The rest is a very well-dressed preamble.

**What convinced me**

- **You found the right problem.** "Seen one pair at a time, these prompts look different... nobody spots the chain by hand." That is the most accurate sentence anyone has written about my job in years. My coaches spend their December holding 20 prompts in their heads and trying to see that the Tufts "favorite assignment" essay is also most of a Penn curiosity essay. Coach Jamal has 12 students with 15+ supplements this week and is, in his word, drowning.
- **The chain diagram on page 5** (Tufts to Penn to Emory to Rice) is the first picture of essay reuse I've seen that I would show a coach. My Airtable can tell me two prompts are both "intellectual curiosity". It can't tell me what order to write them in.
- **Choosing among prompt options** ("Villanova 1 of 5, Emory 1 of 4") is real coaching judgment, and it's usually done badly at 11 p.m.
- **The method sounds careful:** prompts copied word for word for 2026-27, every pair scored in both directions, a length cap ("a 150-word answer cannot fill a 500-word essay"). Someone has actually written a supplement.
- **"No new essays" next to each list suggestion** on page 3 is the only part of the odds section I'd use. Saying "add URI, no supplement" lets a coach make a list suggestion without adding to the student's essay load.

**What worries me (voice, reuse, and plain risk)**

1. **The plan optimizes the wrong thing.** "The plan picks the option that needs the fewest new words." Philip, no. The fewest new words is how you get the dullest application. I pick prompt options by which one draws the best story out of *this* child. Fewer words should break ties, not make the choice.
2. **"Share covered" reads like copy-paste.** "Villanova: a life lesson, 63% from Fordham: a new perspective" will be read by a seventeen-year-old as "paste 158 words and change the ending." Each school only sees its own essays, so that isn't fatal. But a story that gets passed down a four-essay chain flattens like a game of telephone: by Rice, it's a summary of a summary. I need the tool to say *what* is borrowed: the anecdote, the research notes, or actual sentences.
3. **Why-us essays shouldn't be borrowed from at all.** "Rice: why Rice, 53% from Boston University: why BU" is exactly what admissions readers catch, and exactly what I tell students never to do. Find-and-replace on the school name is the oldest sin in the trade. That row should carry a warning, not a recommendation.
4. **The Common App essay is missing.** My inbox this week has a student asking whether her Why Penn essay can reuse the robotics story from her main essay. Your map doesn't include the personal statement, which is both the biggest source for reuse and the biggest risk of repeating yourself. Nor does it check that one school doesn't hear the same story twice. (Penn gets three essays from John. Are they three different Johns, or the same John three times?)
5. **Who are the "two independent scorers"?** Page 7 also says inputs were checked "by an independent agent". In 2026, "agent" means software. If your scorers are language models, say so plainly. I won't put anything near my students that even resembles AI-written essays. My brand, and my clients' trust, rest on "a human helped you find your own voice." The report never says the tool doesn't write. Please say it in large type.
6. **How much does it cover?** One fictional student, 14 schools and 393 pairs. My own Airtable base is at 94% of its record limit, so you can guess how many prompts are in it. If your map is smaller than mine, it's a nice demo and not a tool. Tell me how many schools you have *today*.
7. **The numbers don't quite match.** The heatmap says 62 where the table says 63% (Villanova from Fordham), and 52 where the table says 53% (Rice from BU). The page 5 title says "five strong links let John borrow 42%", but the 42% comes from all eleven rows, not five links. I proofread for a living, and parents will too.
8. **What would my coaches actually do with it?** Google Docs, Airtable and Zoom are my world. Is this a website? Can I upload 240 students' lists from a spreadsheet? A coach won't open a separate tool per student in the middle of the sprint.

**What I'd ask you on the call**

- Who or what are the scorers, and does any part of Calliper generate or rewrite student prose? (If it does, this is a short call.)
- How many schools and prompts are mapped today, and when will the top 100 be done?
- Can it take a student's Common App story and flag supplements that would repeat it?
- Can "share covered" be split into "same story", "same research" and "same sentences", with why-us essays excluded?
- Can I import from, or export to, Airtable or CSV, and run a batch of students at once?
- Has anyone measured coach time saved, rather than words saved? Words are the student's cost. Coach hours are mine.

**Would I try it in the December Sprint?**

Yes, in a small way, and only if it costs me nothing and needs no setup. I'd take Jamal's 12 heaviest students (15+ supplements each) and have one coach compare your essay plan with her own for a week. If the plan finds chains she missed and doesn't push students toward identical essays, we'll keep talking. The Jan 1 crunch is not the time for me to learn software.

**Would I pay, and how much?**

Not today. The report sells odds, which I don't buy, and essay coverage I can't yet judge against my own base.

If the pilot shows it saves at least an hour of coach time per heavy-supplement student, *and* the prompt map covers the top 100 schools with human-checked scores, I would pay **$1,200 to $1,800 a year for a studio license** covering all coaches and unlimited students. Most likely I'd buy before the May bootcamps. I won't pay per student above about $5, because my margins are already under pressure from the very AI tools I'm trying to look different from. I'd also be interested in a data partnership: my prompt base for your scoring, in exchange for a discount and credit.

You've built a very clever map. Now make it a map of *voices*, not just of words.

Onward,
Maggie

## Part 2: Out of character. The 5 changes that would most raise Maggie's willingness to act

1. **Make a coach edition that puts the essay plan first and the odds last.** Put the essay plan on page 1 and move pages 1 to 4 to a one-page appendix. Open with: *"John has 11 supplements due in January, 2,700 words. Written in this order, he drafts 1,576 new words. Your coach reviews 7 essays from scratch instead of 11."* Lead with coach effort, not admission odds.

2. **Add a "No AI writing" promise, and say who the scorers are.** Add a box on page 5 that reads: *"Calliper never writes, rewrites or suggests a sentence. It maps which prompts can share a story or research. The student writes every word."* On page 7, state exactly who the two scorers are. If they're humans, give their background (for example, "two former admissions readers"). If they're models, say so and describe the human check. Replace "independent agent" with plain language.

3. **Split "Share covered" into types of reuse, and block why-us borrowing.** Replace the single percentage with three labels: *Same story / Same research / Same sentences (never recommended)*. Add a rule and show it on the Rice row: *"Why-us essays borrow research notes only: 'Rice: why Rice' reuses your BU course research, not your BU paragraphs."* Also change the plan rule to *"picks the prompt option where the student's strongest story fits best; fewer new words breaks ties."*

4. **Add the Common App essay as a node, and add a same-story check for each school.** Add the personal statement to the chain diagram and the heatmap. Add a check: *"Penn will read 3 supplements + your Common App: 4 different stories ✓"*, plus a warning such as *"Why Penn reuses your Common App robotics story: pick another."* This directly answers the question her students ask every week.

5. **State coverage and the studio workflow, and fix the number mismatches.** Add a line saying how much is covered: *"Prompt map today: N schools, M prompts, all 2026-27; top 100 by [date]."* Add *"Import student lists from CSV or Airtable; export the essay plan to Google Docs; run a whole cohort at once."* Add a pilot offer: *"Free December pilot for up to 15 students; studio licence $1,500/yr."* Fix the heatmap and table numbers (62/63, 52/53) and the page 5 title ("five strong links" versus 42% from all 11 rows), because a proofreader buyer reads mismatches as carelessness.
